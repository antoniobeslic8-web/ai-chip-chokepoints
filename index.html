How it works, and what it can't tell you

Every line in the graph is a real business relationship, taken from my research notes or from the companies' own filings and saved with the link and the exact sentence that proves it, so a script can check that nothing is missing.

The model is built in Python with networkx and works like a chain reaction: when a company stops, any customer that can't buy the same product elsewhere gets stuck, and the problem moves on down the chain to that customer's own customers.

  • It counts companies, not chips. A score is the number of companies left stuck rather than the share of AI chips lost, which is why TSMC, whose stop would hit NVIDIA and AMD, scores below suppliers that hit many smaller firms.
  • Yes or no, nothing in between. The model only knows whether a customer could switch supplier, not how much spare capacity exists, so a supplier with about 90% of its market is treated as the only option.
  • Many answers are unknown. When no source says whether a customer could switch, I run the result both ways, assuming it could and assuming it couldn't, and only trust what holds in both.
  • Big buyers look safer than they are. Microsoft, Meta, Google and Amazon are never hit in the optimistic case because they buy from several chip makers, even though losing NVIDIA and AMD together would leave them badly short.
  • Some companies have no links yet. 19 of the 98 companies have no confirmed links because their filings don't name their customers, and China is probably under-counted for the same reason.
  • Where the sources come from. Most links come from news articles, while the most important ones are backed by the companies' official filings, such as 10-K, 20-F and Japanese annual reports.
